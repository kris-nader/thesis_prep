Try this

Load Bliss-additive. Why does HSA still call synergy?

Every combination equals its Bliss expectation, so Bliss reads 0. HSA compares only against the stronger drug and ignores what the weaker one adds, so plain additivity scores as synergy.

Load Inactive partner and compare the two expected values.

When B does nothing alone, Bliss expects 20 + 0 − 0 = 20, the same as HSA's max(20, 0). The models only disagree when both drugs have an effect on their own.

Load Different max effects and click each well.

Drug A plateaus at 40%. At B 10 nM, drug A sets the HSA bar; at B 100 nM, drug B does. HSA picks whichever measured value is higher in that well, so the plateau never breaks it.

Make HSA call antagonism.

Set a combination below its best single drug, for example A 100 nM + B 100 nM = 55 while B alone at 100 nM is 60.

Try to make Bliss expect less than HSA.

You can't with values between 0 and 100. Under Bliss the weaker drug always adds something, so Bliss expected ≥ HSA expected and every Bliss score ≤ its HSA score.

Sham test: drug A combined with itself

A at dose d plus A at dose d is just A at 2d. Nothing interacts, so a sound null model should score 0. IC50 is fixed at 1 µM.

Try this

Keep h = 1 and drag the dose across the whole range.

Bliss stays negative everywhere. A slope-1 curve has diminishing returns, so doubling the dose always delivers less than Bliss expects. Loewe stays at 0.

Set h = 2 and find where Bliss changes sign.

At about 1.41 µM, which is √2 × IC50. Below it the drug looks synergistic with itself; above it, antagonistic.

Push h to 4 and set d near 0.8 µM.

Bliss reports roughly +37 pp of synergy between a drug and itself. A steep curve alone can fake a large score.

Switch the curve to Exponential.

Bliss now reads 0 at every dose, like Loewe. Exponential survival, where each unit of drug kills the same fraction of the cells still alive, is the only shape on which Bliss and Loewe agree that nothing interacts. HSA still reads positive.

Why is HSA never negative in this test?

HSA expects E(d), and the observed value is E(2d). On any rising curve E(2d) ≥ E(d), so the extra drug alone shows up as synergy.

Loewe: how much of each drug alone would do the same job?

Pick one combination well and the effect you measured in it. Loewe reads each single-drug curve backwards to find the dose that would reach that effect alone, then adds up what fraction of those doses you actually used.

Try this

Drag the observed effect until CI = 1.00.

The well lands on the additive line and the Loewe score is about 0. Below the line means less drug did the job, so synergy; above it, antagonism.

Raise the observed effect and watch the line.

A higher effect needs more of each drug alone, so DA and DB grow, the line moves outward, and the same doses count as more synergistic.

Lower drug B's maximum effect below the observed effect.

Drug B alone can never reach that level, so DB doesn't exist, the line has no end on the B axis, and the CI is undefined. This is the breakdown with different maximum effects.

Compare the three scores in the results table.

They answer different questions. Loewe asks about dose equivalence, Bliss about independent probabilities, HSA about the best single drug, so one well can get three different scores.

ZIP: does drug B shift drug A's curve?

Hold drug B at one dose and look at drug A's dose-response on top of it. If the drugs don't interact, A keeps its own IC50 and slope and simply starts from B's effect. ZIP fits the measured curve and compares it with that expectation. The difference is δ.

How the wells were simulated. At B's full effect, A's IC50 is divided by 10shift. Positive means B makes A more potent.

    Try this

    Set the potency shift to 0.

    The fitted curve lands exactly on ZIP's expectation and δ is 0 at every dose. Drug A kept its own IC50 and slope; it just started from B's effect.

    Move the shift positive, then negative.

    A positive shift moves the fitted curve left (lower IC50): B made A more potent and δ turns positive. A negative shift moves it right and δ turns negative.

    Add 5 pp of noise and press New noise a few times.

    Raw Bliss jumps around from well to well because it scores each noisy point. δ moves much less because it scores the fitted curve. That smoothing is the main practical reason to use ZIP.

    Keep the shift and hold B at 0.1, then 3, then 10 µM.

    At 0.1 µM drug B barely acts, so it barely shifts A and δ is tiny. At 10 µM the shift is largest, but B alone already gives about 90%, leaving little room above it, so δ shrinks again. The biggest δ sits at intermediate doses.

    Try this

    Start with Independent and compare the four matrices.

    Bliss and ZIP read 0 in every well, because the wells were filled with their own idea of no interaction. HSA reads positive and Loewe is non-zero: each model defines "no interaction" differently.

    Switch to Same drug.

    Loewe reads 0 everywhere, since a drug with itself is dose-additive by definition. Bliss and ZIP go negative at slope 1, and HSA stays positive.

    Choose Potency shift and move the strength.

    All four respond, each from its own baseline. Without noise, ZIP and Bliss agree almost exactly, because ZIP's expected value is Bliss. ZIP's advantage shows up once you add noise.

    Under Independent, lower drug B's maximum effect to 60%.

    Bliss stays at 0. ZIP drifts away from 0 because it assumes the refitted combination curves rise to 100%. Loewe can't be computed in wells where drug A alone already exceeds B's 60% ceiling.

    Add 5 pp of noise.

    HSA, Bliss and Loewe change from well to well with the noise. ZIP is smoother because it scores fitted curves rather than single wells.
